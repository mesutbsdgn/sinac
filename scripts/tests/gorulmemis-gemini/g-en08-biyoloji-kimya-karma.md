# Enzyme Kinetics, Cellular Respiration, and Buffer pH

## 1. Enzyme Catalysis and Michaelis–Menten Kinetics
Enzymes are biological protein catalysts that lower the activation energy of biochemical reactions without shifting the thermodynamic equilibrium. In Michaelis–Menten kinetics, $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which reaction velocity reaches half of $V_{\max}$.

## 2. Cellular Respiration and Glucose Concentration
During aerobic respiration, one mol of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) is oxidized to six mol of $\text{CO}_2$ and six mol of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, driving ATP synthesis across glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. In physiological studies, a postprandial glucose level of 108 mg/dL (6.0 mmol/L) is commonly used as a classroom conversion example.

## 3. Buffer Solutions and Henderson–Hasselbalch Equation
A buffer resists changes in pH upon addition of small amounts of strong acid or base. Using a 1.0 mol/L acetic acid and sodium acetate buffer at 25 °C ($pK_a = 4.76$):
$$\text{pH} = pK_a + \log_{10}\left(\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}\right)$$
When $[\text{A}^-] = [\text{HA}]$, the solution pH equals 4.76. In laboratory work, wear protective gloves and eye protection (GHS P280) when handling concentrated acids.

## Self-Check
1. Does an enzyme change the equilibrium constant of a reaction?
2. What is the pH of a buffer when the conjugate base and weak acid concentrations are equal?

### Answers
1. No; it only accelerates the rate at which equilibrium is reached.
2. $\text{pH} = pK_a$.
