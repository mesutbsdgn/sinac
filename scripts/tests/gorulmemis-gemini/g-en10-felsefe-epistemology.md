# Introduction to Epistemology: Sources of Knowledge

## 1. The Classical Tripartite Analysis
Epistemology examines the nature, scope, and justification of human knowledge. Following Plato's *Theaetetus*, knowledge has traditionally been analyzed as justified true belief (JTB), though twentieth-century counterexamples by Edmund Gettier (1963) showed that accidental justification can undermine this definition.

## 2. Rationalism vs. Empiricism
- **Rationalism** (e.g., René Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, 1641) holds that reason alone can yield substantive *a priori* truths independent of sensory experience.
- **Empiricism** (e.g., David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, 1748) maintains that all knowledge of matters of fact originates in sensory impressions. Hume famously challenged induction by noting that observing an object move at 2.0 m/s in past trials does not logically guarantee identical future behavior.

## 3. Kant's Critical Synthesis
Immanuel Kant (born 22 April 1724, Königsberg; died 1804) argued in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) that experience requires both sensory intuition and *a priori* concepts of the understanding. Neither raw sensation nor pure logic alone constitutes empirical knowledge.

## Self-Check
1. What three conditions make up the classical tripartite definition of knowledge?
2. How did Kant attempt to bridge rationalism and empiricism?

### Answers
1. Belief, truth, and justification.
2. By arguing that knowledge combines sensory input with *a priori* mental structures (space, time, and categories).
