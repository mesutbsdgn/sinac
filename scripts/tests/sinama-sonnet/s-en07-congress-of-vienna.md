# Modern European History: The Congress of Vienna, 1814–1815

## 1. Background

Napoleon abdicated in April 1814, and the First Treaty of Paris (30 May 1814) restored the Bourbon monarchy under Louis XVIII. The victorious powers then met in Vienna to settle the continent. The Congress opened in the autumn of 1814 and its Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815.

## 2. Who was there

| Statesman | Power | Age at the opening of the Congress |
|---|---|---|
| Prince Klemens von Metternich | Austria | 41 |
| Viscount Castlereagh | Britain | 45 |
| Tsar Alexander I | Russia | 36 |
| Prince von Hardenberg | Prussia | 64 |
| Charles-Maurice de Talleyrand | France | 60 |

The 60-year-old Talleyrand, representing a defeated country, skilfully turned the doctrine of legitimacy to France's advantage and was admitted to the inner negotiations. The 58-year-old Louis XVIII, back on the throne, relied on him entirely. Britain's military hero, the 45-year-old Duke of Wellington, replaced Castlereagh at the table in early 1815.

## 3. Guiding principles

- **Legitimacy:** restore the dynasties that Napoleon had displaced.
- **Balance of power:** no single state should be able to dominate the continent.
- **Compensation:** territorial changes were offered to the winners to keep them satisfied.

## 4. Main territorial decisions

1. A Kingdom of Poland, linked to Russia by the tsar's crown.
2. The German Confederation of 39 states, under Austrian presidency, replaced the Holy Roman Empire that had ended in 1806.
3. The Kingdom of the Netherlands was created as a buffer on France's northern border.
4. Norway passed from Denmark to Sweden; Switzerland received guaranteed neutrality.
5. Prussia gained the Rhineland and part of Saxony; Austria gained northern Italy.

## 6. The Concert of Europe

After 1815 the great powers met periodically (Aix-la-Chapelle in 1818 and later conferences) to manage crises collectively. The system suppressed revolutions in Spain and Naples but could not stop nationalism and liberalism; it eroded after 1848.

## 7. Historians' debate

Henry Kissinger's 1957 book *A World Restored* praised the settlement for producing a long period without a general war. Paul Schroeder, writing in 1994, argued that the real stability came from new rules of restraint among the powers rather than from a simple balance. Critics emphasise that the settlement ignored the wishes of peoples, especially in Poland, Italy and Belgium.

## 8. Effects

- A century of relative peace among the great powers (1815–1914), interrupted only by limited wars
- Conservative order in central and eastern Europe
- Seeds of later national unification movements in Germany and Italy
