# Introductory Economics: Elasticity, Multipliers and Interest Rates

## 1. Price elasticity of demand

Elasticity measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a price change:

E = %change in quantity ÷ %change in price

| Absolute value of E | Description | Effect of a price rise on revenue |
|---|---|---|
| above 1 | elastic | revenue falls |
| equal to 1 | unit elastic | revenue unchanged |
| below 1 | inelastic | revenue rises |

### Midpoint example

The price rises from 10 to 12 and quantity falls from 100 to 90.

- %change in Q = −10 ÷ 95 = −10.5%
- %change in P = 2 ÷ 11 = 18.2%
- E = −10.5 ÷ 18.2 = −0.58, so demand is inelastic.
- Revenue moves from 10 × 100 = 1,000 to 12 × 90 = 1,080.

## 2. Linear demand

For the demand curve Q = 200 − 4P, the point elasticity is E = −4 × P ÷ Q.

| Price | Quantity | Elasticity | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 120 | −0.67 | 2,400 |
| 25 | 100 | −1.00 | 2,500 |
| 30 | 80 | −1.50 | 2,400 |

Revenue is highest at P = 25, where demand is unit elastic. That is a general rule for linear demand.

## 3. The expenditure multiplier

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume.

- MPC = 0.8 gives a multiplier of 5; a government spending increase of 20 raises output by 100.
- MPC = 0.75 gives a multiplier of 4; an increase of 50 raises output by 200.
- Taxes and imports leak out of the spending chain, so real-world multipliers are smaller.

## 4. Nominal versus real interest

Real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation (Fisher approximation). With a nominal rate of 5.0% and inflation of 2.0%, the real rate is about 3.0%.

An exact calculation uses (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 = 1.05 ÷ 1.02 − 1 = 2.94%.

## 5. Inflation and growth arithmetic

- CPI rising from 250 to 260 means inflation of 10 ÷ 250 = 4%.
- GDP deflator = nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100 = 550 ÷ 500 × 100 = 110.
- Rule of 70: at 3.5% annual growth, output doubles in about 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 years.

## 6. A short intellectual history

John Maynard Keynes published *The General Theory* in 1936, when the 52-year-old economist argued that spending shortfalls could keep economies below full employment. Much of this note's multiplier logic descends from that book. Classical economists, by contrast, expected markets to clear without government spending.

## 7. Applying the ideas

- A transit authority that raises fares by 10% faces inelastic demand if commuters have no alternative; revenue rises.
- A tax on a good with elastic demand mostly reduces consumption and raises little revenue.
- A temporary stimulus of 1,000 million in a recession with MPC 0.6 yields a multiplier of 2.5 and raises output by about 2,500 million.
